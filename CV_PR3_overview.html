Teaching a Computer to Find Faces and Objects

This project teaches a computer to look at a photo and understand what's in it. It has five parts. The first two use simple pixel-level tricks — no AI yet. The last three bring in two trained AI models, ending with both working together on one photo. Here is what each part covers.

Part 1 of 5

Cleaning up images

Before any AI, we clean and reshape images using simple pixel filters. These filters only understand black and white, but they are the building blocks for everything after.

Comparing 9 different filter settings on a noisy image. The cleanest result (top-left) uses a small, simple filter.

  • Turning a photo into pure black & white (binary)
  • Erode (shrink white areas) and dilate (grow white areas)
  • Open (remove small noise) and close (fill small holes)
  • Comparing filter shapes and sizes to find the best one

Part 2 of 5

Masking, logic & histograms

Next, we combine shapes using simple logic, cut regions out of real photos, and read an image's brightness as a graph.

Combining a circle and a rectangle mask with four logic operations.

  • Combining two shapes with AND, OR, XOR, NOT
  • Cutting a round region out of a real photo (masking)
  • Adding a watermark on top of a photo
  • Plotting a histogram to see how bright or dark a photo is
  • Adjusting brightness and contrast, and watching the histogram shift

Part 3 of 5

Finding faces

Now the first AI model: YuNet, trained only to find human faces. We test how well it copes with sunglasses and faces turned away from the camera.

Left: two faces found easily. Right: zero faces found — both people are turned away from the camera.

  • Loading a small face-detection AI model (YuNet)
  • Testing it on different face angles and sunglasses
  • Measuring detection speed (frames per second)
  • Trying stricter and looser confidence settings
  • Blurring each detected face for privacy

Part 4 of 5

Finding objects

A second AI model: YOLOv8n, trained to recognize 80 kinds of everyday objects, not just faces.

YOLO labels every object it recognizes, with a confidence score for each.

  • Loading YOLOv8n, a general object detector (80 classes)
  • Testing it on real photos
  • Measuring detection speed (frames per second)
  • Changing how confident it must be before showing a box
  • Cleaning up duplicate overlapping boxes (Non-Max Suppression)
  • Counting how many of each object type it found

Part 5 of 5

Putting it together

Finally, both AI models run on the same photo, and we compare every technique from this project side by side.

The final comparison table: what each technique is good at, and how fast it runs.

  • Drawing face boxes (green) and object boxes (red) on one photo
  • Checking if cleaning a noisy photo first actually helps
  • Comparing speed: face-only vs. object-only vs. both together
  • Building one final table comparing every technique

What we found

  • A small 3×3 filter cleaned noise better than a bigger one — bigger is not always better.
  • Sunglasses didn't stop the face detector, but a face turned away from the camera did.
  • Running both AI models together is slower than running just one, as expected.

Built with Python, OpenCV, and YOLOv8.
